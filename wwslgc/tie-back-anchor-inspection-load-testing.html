apartment What a tie-back anchor is — and why it fails quietly

A tie-back anchor is the rooftop anchorage a suspended worker’s line ties back to — the eyebolt, U-anchor, or davit base that holds a window washer, a rope-access technician, or a swing-stage outrigger tieback. It is the single point between the worker and a fall. Tie-backs fail quietly: a bolt corrodes inside the embedment, a base plate works loose after a re-roof, a weld cracks under years of cyclic load — and it all looks fine from three feet away. That is why a visual walk-around is not a certification. We inspect, proof-load, and certify tie-back anchors across South Florida so the crew on your facade is tied to something that has actually been proven.

water_drop Oceanfront salt air corrodes anchors from the inside

An anchor on a coastal tower in Palm Beach, Fort Lauderdale or Miami lives a harder life than one inland. Chloride-laden air drives corrosion of anchor bolts, base plates, and concrete embedments long before a rust stain shows — the connection can look perfect and still fail a pull test. Our crews proof-load every tie-back, inspect the substrate, and document the result so hidden corrosion is caught before it becomes a fall. Anything with permanent deflection greater than 1/16 inch under load fails and is tagged out of service on the spot.

cleaning_services Window-washing and facade-access anchorage

Any South Florida building where workers suspend from the roof to clean glass or service the facade relies on certified anchorage. Rope-descent-system (RDS) anchors must be certified to hold the loads set in OSHA 1910.27(b), and tieback anchors are proof-loaded under ANSI/IWCA I-14.1 to 2,500 lbs static - twice the 1,250 lb allowable service load. Davit posts and arms under ASME A120.1 are tested to 2x their working load. We handle roof anchor load testing and window-washing anchor certification across South Florida and Palm Beach County for davit bases, tieback anchors, and continuous lifeline systems alike. verified What OSHA actually requires - and what it costs to skip

The rules are specific. Under OSHA 1910.27(b), rope-descent anchorages must be inspected by a qualified person at least annually and certified at intervals no greater than 10 years - sooner if a problem is found - and the building owner has to keep that certification on file and hand it to contractors before they work. Personal fall-arrest anchors fall under 1910.140 and require a competent-person check before each use. Ignore it and 2026 OSHA penalties run up to $16,550 per serious violation and up to $165,514 for a willful or repeat citation - per item, before any injury liability or insurance fallout. For a South Florida property manager, an uncertified anchor is both a life-safety and a six-figure financial exposure.

event_repeat Annual inspection, 10-year certification — and the re-roof reset

Two clocks run on every tie-back anchor. Under OSHA 1910.27(b) rope-descent anchorages must be inspected by a qualified person at least annually and certified at intervals no greater than 10 years. Under ANSI/IWCA I-14.1 the certification is a proof load to 2,500 lbs — twice the 1,250 lb allowable service load. And a third event resets both: any re-roof, restoration, or structural work that disturbs the anchors voids the existing certification until they are re-tested. If your building re-roofed recently, its tie-backs are almost certainly uncertified right now. See our re-roof anchor certification page and the visual inspection vs. load test guide.
