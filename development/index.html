Inside the Work

What happens at each stage.

01

Source

Opportunities come from reading a market rather than reacting to a listing. We look for parcels and buildings whose constraints have kept better-capitalized buyers away, then test whether those constraints are solvable.

  • Define the buy box before looking, not after
  • Walk the site and the block, not just the map
  • Confirm ownership, access, and title posture early
  • Kill weak opportunities fast so good ones get attention
02

Underwrite

Underwriting is where enthusiasm meets arithmetic. We build the case on what the site can actually support — physically, legally, and financially — and we write down the conditions that would make us walk away before we are attached to the deal.

  • Model the buildable envelope, not the wished-for one
  • Price the regulatory path, including the time it takes
  • Stress the assumptions that would hurt most if wrong
  • Record the walk-away test in writing
03

Structure

Structure is how the plan survives contact with reality. Control of the property, the capital behind it, and the responsibilities of everyone touching it get defined before work begins — so nobody is negotiating roles while the clock runs.

  • Secure control on terms that match the approval timeline
  • Match the capital stack to the risk each party is taking
  • Set the decision rights and reporting cadence up front
  • Sequence approvals so no consultant waits on another
04

Execute

Execution is coordination under a schedule. Licensed design and construction professionals do the regulated work; our job is to keep the sequence honest, surface problems while they are still small, and make sure decisions get made by the person who owns them.

  • Run one schedule that every party works from
  • Hold a standing cadence for issues and approvals
  • Track changes against the underwriting, not against hope
  • Close out documentation as the work completes
05

Exit

The exit is designed at the beginning, not discovered at the end. We name who the property is for on the other side of the work and keep the plan pointed at that outcome, so the handoff is a scheduled event rather than a scramble.

  • Define the end holder before committing capital
  • Keep the record clean enough to hand over cleanly
  • Re-test the exit whenever the market moves
  • Report results against what was underwritten

Underwriting

How an opportunity gets evaluated.

Before a property earns capital, it has to earn a yes on every line below. Any single unresolved answer is enough to stop the process — that is the point of having a list.

Evaluation criteria applied to every development opportunity
Site & Parcel Boundaries, access, orientation, and how existing conditions constrain what can realistically be built.
Regulatory Path Which approvals the intended use requires, who grants them, and how long that path actually runs.
Physical Conditions Soils, drainage, utilities, and elevation — the conditions that quietly change the cost of building anything.
Cost & Capital What the work costs to deliver, how it gets funded, and which party carries which risk at each draw.
Market Fit Who the finished property is for, and whether the plan serves real demand rather than a preference.
Exit Path The intended outcome and what has to remain true for that outcome to still be available at completion.
Walk-Away Test The conditions under which we stop — written before diligence starts, not rationalized afterward.

Accountability

Built to execute.

Development is a coordination problem before it is a construction problem. Most of the cost and most of the delay is created in the handoffs — between the plan and the permit, the permit and the field, the field and the closeout.

We stay in the middle of those handoffs from the first walk of a site through final documentation. One party keeps the sequence, holds the schedule, and answers for how the process ran.

Development discipline. Operating clarity. One accountable partner.
