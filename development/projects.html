Development Concept

The Range House — Pensacola, FL.

Three businesses under one roof. Part lodging, part entertainment venue, part corporate retreat — sold to groups who want one roof, an all-weather activity, and a buy-the-whole-place experience.

An adaptive reuse of an existing 40,688 SF building on 13.5 acres in Pensacola's growing I-110 corridor: roughly 24 group suites wrapped around eight golf-simulator bays, a bar and lounge, catering-ready event space, and an outdoor skate park on the balance of the site. Adaptive reuse gives the project a lower-cost basis than ground-up.

The demand already drives here — golf trips, NAS Pensacola and its 53,000+ jobs, aerospace growth in the corridor including ST Engineering's MRO expansion and the 1,700 jobs it adds, and Navy Federal's roughly 10,000 employees minutes away.

Collaborative Concept sources, underwrites, and structures the deal. Construction is carried by our licensed GC partner, Legally Construction.

How to read the status labels

Status describes where a project stands inside our own process. It is not a marketing stage and it is not a prediction.

Completed work is listed with the year it was delivered and the licensed contractor that built it. A concept is labelled a concept, and its numbers are the base case out of its feasibility package rather than a result.

If a filter above returns nothing, that is the honest answer: there is no project at that stage we can describe publicly right now.

Status labels used on this page and what each one means
Active — Raising Capital The plan is set and we are assembling the capital to execute it. This is the stage where partner conversations happen.
Concept Feasibility, market, and cost analysis complete or underway. Base-case numbers come from that work, and the open diligence gates are named.
Completed Delivered work, listed with the year it was completed and the licensed contractor that built it.
Not listed Everything else: opportunities we are screening, opportunities we passed on, and work we cannot describe without the agreement of the people involved.

What we evaluate

Every opportunity is screened against the same questions. They are deliberately unglamorous — most of what goes wrong on a project this size is visible before anyone signs anything.

Geography

We work where we can be on site the same day. On projects this size, concentration beats reach — proximity is what makes direct management possible.

The constraint

Every project we take on has something wrong with it that a plan can fix. If nothing is wrong, we are not the party who should be winning that deal.

Position

Who controls the property today, what the owner actually needs, and whether a structure exists that gets them there without pretending the constraint away.

Approvals path

What the property is allowed to become, what it would take to get there, and who has to say yes. Those determinations are made with the licensed professionals whose judgment governs them — we do not make them ourselves.

Scale

Projects we can manage directly, without adding a layer of people between the decision and the work. We would rather be accountable than large.

Capital and exit

How the project gets funded, what it costs to carry while the plan is proved, and what a realistic outcome looks like before we ask anyone to participate.

How an opportunity moves through review

Four stages. Any one of them can end the conversation — that is the point of running them in this order.

Screen

Location, the constraint, and whether a workable structure is even possible.

Diligence

Test the assumptions with the licensed professionals whose judgment governs them.

Structure

Define who does what, who funds what, and what each party is accountable for.

Decision

Commit and execute, or pass and say plainly why we passed.

Bring us an opportunity

  1. Send the basics

    The parcel or the address, what you control today, and what you think is wrong with it. A paragraph is enough to start.

  2. Tell us what you need

    A development partner, capital, entitlement work, or an exit. The answer changes what we can usefully do and how fast we can do it.

  3. We screen it

    You get a straight answer on whether it fits the criteria above. A pass is still an answer, and you will get the reason with it.

  4. If it fits, we structure it

    The plan, the roles, and the accountability go in writing before anything moves. Nobody should be guessing what they signed up for.
