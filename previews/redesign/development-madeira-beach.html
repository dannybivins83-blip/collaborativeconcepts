Opportunity & Constraints

What makes it worth doing, and what it has to work around.

A project is only as good as the constraints it was designed inside. Both sides are written down here, in the same place, because a plan that only shows the upside is not a plan.

The opportunity

Why the site is worth pursuing

  • Assembled, not fragmented. A double-lot assembly allows one coherent residential plan instead of two compromised ones.
  • Irregular conditions surfaced early. Non-conforming conditions are identified and worked through during diligence rather than left for a later party to discover.
  • Design and resilience carry the outcome. On a coastal site, design quality and resilience are what separate a good outcome from an ordinary one.
  • Single-party accountability. The project is structured so one accountable party carries it end to end without layers of hand-off.
  • A reviewable record. Every assumption traces back to a document a reviewer can open.

The constraints

What the plan has to work around

  • Coastal and floodplain requirements. They shape the building form before any design preference does.
  • Non-conforming review steps. An irregular lot carries review that a conforming site does not.
  • Agency timing. Review schedules sit outside any developer's control and have to be planned around, not assumed.
  • Trade availability. Coastal construction narrows the pool of qualified trades and stretches some lead times.
  • Capital sequencing. Structure has to tolerate a schedule that can move, not one presumed fixed.

Plan & Site Strategy

The envelope comes first. The plan is designed inside it.

Nothing gets drawn before the limits are known. The site's requirements set the buildable envelope, licensed design professionals work inside it, and the two lots are planned together so shared conditions are resolved once instead of twice.

Constraints first

The buildable envelope is derived from the site's requirements before a floor plan exists. Design decisions are made inside a known limit rather than negotiated backwards out of a drawing someone already fell in love with.

Two lots, one plan

Access, grade, drainage, frontage, and staging are shared conditions. Planning the lots together means each is resolved once, coordinated across both, and not rediscovered halfway through the second build.

Reviewable at every step

Drawings, reports, agency correspondence, and cost documents live in one package. A lender, partner, or reviewer sees the same record we work from — no separate internal version.

Risks & Disclosures

What could move, and how the plan accounts for it.

This is a summary of the risk categories we actively manage. It is not the full risk discussion — that lives in the project package, alongside the assumptions each item is measured against.

Approvals and permittingAgency review timing is outside our control. Approval-dependent work is sequenced early so dependencies stay visible rather than surfacing late.
Site and design conditionsField conditions can differ from assumptions. Diligence is completed before commitments are made, and the plan is revised when findings require it.
Construction cost and availabilityPricing and trade availability move. Scopes are defined before they are priced, and contingency is carried in the plan rather than hoped for.
Weather and coastal exposureCoastal work carries seasonal and storm exposure. Sequencing and schedule float are planned for it from the start.
Market conditionsDemand and financing conditions change. The plan is tested against a range of conditions rather than a single favorable case.
Capital and financingAvailability and cost of capital can change. Structure, terms, and reporting cadence are documented and provided on request.
ScheduleMilestones depend on third parties. Progress is reported against the plan of record, not against a best case.
